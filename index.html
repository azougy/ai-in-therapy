§01The access gap

Half of them
got nothing

100 units  =  59.3M adults 49.4% no treatment

Of the 59.3 million U.S. adults with any mental illness in 2022, 50.6% received treatment in the prior year. Treatment here counts medication or counseling.1

Read it carefully

“No treatment” is not the same as unmet demand for AI. Some of that 49.4% were not looking for care. The gap is a reason to study access, not a market size.

The opportunity is narrow: make the first step easier, and keep a clear path to a human after it.1,6,7,12

§03Case example

Tee priced the
first conversation

Talkspace’s AI mental-health guide. Speak or type, any hour, unlimited, $19.99 a month after a seven-day trial. It remembers the last conversation. Talkspace is deliberate that Tee is a guide, not a licensed therapist.18,19

Why it is not a general chatbot

A conversation with a shape

  1. Understandwhat the person wants to talk about
  2. Set a focusfor the conversation
  3. Explorethe thoughts or emotions underneath it
  4. Apply a techniqueincluding CBT-based exercises
  5. Reflecton what came out of it

What it changes

A rung that was not there

Before Tee, the step after “I am struggling” was a licensed professional, or nothing. Traditional therapy is bounded by clinician time: every extra appointment needs capacity, scheduling and cost. Tee separates one layer of support from those constraints.

  • No support
  • AI-guided support  $19.99 / month, unlimited
  • Licensed therapy and psychiatry  diagnosis, treatment, crisis

What this commoditizes, and what it does not. Tee prices one layer of mental-health support like software instead of like clinician hours. It does not commoditize therapy: diagnosis, formal treatment, crisis management and complex clinical judgment stay with licensed professionals, and Talkspace’s own human therapy plans cost substantially more than the AI subscription.19

Escalation path · idle

A flagged conversation goes to a licensed supervising clinician, who can start an escalation pathway. Clinicians sit in the monitoring and escalation system, inside what the filing calls a human-in-the-loop quality management system. Talkspace states there is no real-time human monitoring of every conversation.18,21

A

Domain-specific, not general

Sentia is proprietary and behavioral-health focused. Talkspace also runs systems that check whether a response aligns with what a skilled licensed therapist would say, while still distinguishing Tee from therapy.20,22

B

It carries the thread

Tee remembers previous conversations and adapts to them, so the fifth conversation is not a cold start like the first.18

C

Components, not one call

Several AI components handle different functions and an orchestration layer decides how they work together. One model response is not doing all of this.20

D

Detection plus a human

Automated monitoring flags indicators of elevated clinical risk for a supervising clinician rather than deciding the clinical response itself.21

Before launch

Plan it carefully

  1. Give it one job.Sign-ups, practice between sessions, or note-taking. Not acting as the therapist.
  2. Keep a human close.If someone is at risk, a real clinician steps in right away.
  3. People decide.AI can help; the clinician makes the care decisions.
  4. Start small.One clinic first, then expand what actually worked.
  5. Check real results.Are people feeling better, and getting care sooner?
After launch

Support it properly

  1. Test it on real patients.Across ages, languages and backgrounds.
  2. Keep watching it.Check it regularly after launch and report problems.
  3. Protect private data.Store it safely and limit what the vendor can do with it.
  4. Train the staff.Teach clinicians how to use it, and when not to trust it.
  5. Include everyone.Other languages, and options for people without smartphones.

§06Ethical and legal concerns

Four questions
it has to answer

The checklist before trusting any of these tools with someone’s mental health.

01

Do people know they are talking to AI?

  • Be upfront. Always tell people when they are chatting with a bot.
  • Ask first. Get permission before recording a session for AI notes.
  • Name the limits. Say what it cannot do, and where to find crisis help.
02

Is their private information safe?

  • A legal gap. HIPAA, the main U.S. health privacy law, mostly does not cover apps.
  • Some protection. Since 2024 the FTC requires health apps to report data leaks.
  • The open question. Can your conversations train the model, or target ads at you?
03

Could it cause harm?

  • Bias. Models trained mostly on English, Western data can misread other cultures.
  • Too agreeable. A bot that always agrees can reinforce a harmful thought.
  • Children. In 2025 the FTC asked seven AI companies, OpenAI and Meta included, how they protect kids.
04

Who is responsible when it goes wrong?

  • Few rules. The FDA only steps in when an app claims to treat an illness.
  • Unclear blame. The company, the therapist, or the clinic?
  • Guidelines fill the gap. Groups like the APA set their own standards instead.12

§07Benefits and limitations

Access is the upside.
Evidence is the catch.

137M

Americans live in a mental-health shortage area. HRSA, 2025.

51%

Self-reported drop in depression symptoms in the first generative-AI RCT, n=210.3

0

FDA-authorized generative-AI mental-health devices. FDA, Nov 2025.

What holds up

  • Access. Roughly 1,600 depressed or anxious patients per U.S. provider. A bot is available at 2am, costs little, and has no waitlist.
  • The Therabot RCT. n=210, large effects against a waitlist control, d≈0.9 depression and 0.8 anxiety, all self-reported.3
  • Meta-analysis. 14 RCTs, n=6,314, a moderate pooled effect on depression, g≈0.5, with wide error bars.
  • Engagement. People start sessions themselves, often at night, and rate the bond on par with human therapists.
  • Clinician support. AI note-taking cut burnout by 21 points across 1,430 clinicians in general medicine. Administrative, not clinical.

What does not

  • Crisis failures. Bots have listed tall bridges to a user who had just lost a job. Newer models did no better.
  • Ethics gaps. 15 ethical risks catalogued at Brown. RAND found bots cannot grade mid-level suicide risk.
  • Lawsuits. Character.AI and Google settling five teen suits as of January 2026. OpenAI faces Raine and seven more.
  • Thin evidence. Self-reported outcomes, waitlist controls, about 16% with follow-up. The anxiety benefit is unproven.
  • Oversight. No FDA generative-AI device in this category. Illinois bans AI therapy. BetterHelp paid a $7.8M privacy fine.

§08Where the industry is going

Money is in.
The lanes are drawn.

01

Capital is flowing, market sizing is noise

  • $14.2B across 482 U.S. digital-health deals in 2025, up 35%. AI took 54% of the dollars.
  • Market size. About $1.7B to $1.8B in 2025, projected to $9B to $12B by 2033 or 2034. One vendor report claims $64B already in 2026.
  • Purpose-built models. Slingshot AI raised $93M for its therapy model Ash after a 50,000-user beta.
02

Lanes for tools, a wall for “AI therapists”

  • FDA. Zero generative-AI mental-health devices authorized. The TEMPO pilot admitted its first AI therapy firm in 2026.
  • States. Illinois bans AI therapy at $10k per violation, Nevada bans it, Utah requires disclosure, California passed a crisis law.
  • Abroad. EU high-risk duties slip to 2027 and 2028. The APA, WHO and UK regulators all say support, do not replace.
03

Frontier labs are crisis responders by default

  • OpenAI. Says 0.15% of weekly users show signs of suicidal planning. 170 or more clinicians involved, and 65% to 80% fewer bad replies claimed.
  • Anthropic and Google. A real-time self-harm classifier, and Gemini linked to crisis lines with $30M pledged.
  • Character.AI. Barred under-18s in November 2025. 19% of U.S. youth ask chatbots for mental-health advice.
04

“Augment” is winning, “replace” is lab-bound

  • Augment. Lyra, Spring Health and Talkspace route at-risk users to human clinicians, on company-reported numbers.
  • Adoption. Psychologists who never use AI fell from 71% to 44%. Medicare still has no AI reimbursement policy.
  • Replace. Therabot is research-only, Limbic’s data is company-reported, and Woebot shut down in 2025.

§09Conclusion and discussion

Where we land

The same split shows up in every section. These systems are good at conversation, paperwork and routing. They are weak at judging risk. The products that work in 2026 pick one of those jobs and keep a clinician attached to it.

That is what makes Tee worth looking at. Talkspace priced the supportive-conversation layer at $19.99 a month and left diagnosis, treatment and crisis care with licensed people. The lawsuits in the news are not about products built that way. They are about general chatbots that millions of people used for mental-health support with no clinical design behind them.

And none of it is approved as treatment. Zero generative-AI mental-health devices have FDA authorization. The evidence is one promising trial, a meta-analysis with wide error bars, and a large pile of company-reported numbers.

Discussion

Tee has no waitlist and costs $19.99 a month. If that is good enough for the first conversation, who decides when someone moves up to a licensed clinician?

The user decides

The case

People already self-triage. A tool with no queue lowers the cost of asking for help early, which is exactly where the access gap sits.

The problem

The person in the worst shape is the least likely to escalate, and the business earns more the longer someone stays in the cheap layer.

The risk model decides

The case

It reads every conversation, never gets tired, and the escalation path to a supervising clinician is already built and running.

The problem

RAND found these models cannot grade mid-level suicide risk, and the company selling the subscription is the one setting the threshold.

The payer decides

The case

Insurers and employers hold the money. They can require a human after a set number of sessions, and they already gate the rest of care.

The problem

Medicare has no AI reimbursement policy at all. The cheap layer stays out of pocket, and the incentive runs toward whatever costs least.
