  • 59.3 million U.S. adults had any mental illness in 2022. 50.6% received treatment in the prior year.
  • Treatment here means medication or counseling. It is not a measure of unmet demand for AI, and some of that 49.4% were not seeking care.
  • Three barriers. Cost and coverage, provider capacity, and the stigma and friction of booking a first appointment.
  • Where AI is pointed. Finding care options, intake and workflow support, and a low-pressure first conversation.

02Why people do not get care

Cost

Affordable care is hard to find, and coverage decides who gets seen.

Capacity

Clinician hours are a fixed supply. Every extra session needs a person.

Stigma

Finding a provider and booking the first appointment can feel like too much.

AI is aimed at the first step, not the treatment
  • Cost and coverage. AI is being pointed at helping people find care options they can afford.
  • Provider capacity. Roughly 1,600 depressed or anxious patients per U.S. provider. AI is used for intake and workflow, which frees clinician time.
  • Stigma and uncertainty. A chatbot is a low-pressure first conversation for someone not ready to call a clinic.
  • The honest framing. Make the first step easier and keep a clear path to a human after it.

Drafting notes and giving advice are not the same product
  • Self-help and skills. Wysa and Youper run guided conversations and coping exercises on demand. A helpful interaction does not prove clinical benefit.
  • Care navigation. Limbic and Lyra's AI guide handle intake, guidance and referral in front of a care system.
  • Clinician support. Eleos drafts notes and session feedback for a clinician to review.
  • Treatment research. The Therabot trial is AI-led therapeutic dialogue inside a clinical study, n=210.
  • Human-led digital care. Ieso, now Mindler, is a human therapist delivering CBT online. It gets miscounted as AI care.
  • The line to hold. Product features and user engagement do not establish clinical effectiveness.

04Case example · Talkspace

Tee, Talkspace’s AI mental-health guide

$19.99

A month. Unlimited conversations, any hour, after a seven-day trial.19

A guide.
Not a licensed therapist.

Speak or type · It remembers the last conversationTalkspace
  • What it is. A private place to talk through stress, relationships, anxiety or a hard decision, 24/7, by voice or text.
  • Pricing. $19.99 a month, unlimited, after a seven-day free trial. Talkspace's human therapy plans cost substantially more.
  • Positioning. Talkspace does not call Tee a licensed therapist. It is a guide, with a path to professional care when someone needs more.
  • Session shape. Understand the concern, set a focus, explore what is underneath, apply a CBT-based exercise, reflect. More structure than a general chatbot.
  • Memory. It remembers previous conversations and builds on them, so the fifth conversation is not a cold start.

No queue, flat price
It commoditizes one layer of support, not therapy
  • The constraint. Traditional therapy is bounded by clinician time. Every extra appointment needs capacity, scheduling and payment.
  • What Tee separates. The supportive-conversation layer comes out of that constraint and gets priced like software.
  • What it does not commoditize. Diagnosis, formal treatment, crisis management and complex clinical judgment stay with licensed professionals.
  • The new ladder. No support, then AI-guided support, then licensed therapy and psychiatry. Tee is the middle rung that did not exist.
  • Say the number. $19.99 a month against human therapy plans that cost substantially more out of pocket.

  • The point of the slide. Tee does not replace a therapist. It fills the space between nothing and a clinician.
  • Why that matters. Most of the 49.4% were never going to reach the third column. The middle rung is cheap enough to reach.

Risk detection flags the conversation for a licensed supervising clinician, who can open an escalation pathway. Talkspace states there is no real-time human monitoring of every conversation.18,21

  • Not a general model with a wrapper. Sentia is Talkspace's own behavioral-health model, trained on clinical data from millions of patient journeys, per their SEC filing.
  • Multi-agent orchestrator. Several components handle different functions and an orchestration layer decides how they work together. One model call is not doing all of this.
  • Quality checks. Talkspace runs systems that evaluate whether a response aligns with what a skilled licensed therapist would say, while still distinguishing Tee from therapy.
  • Safety architecture. Automated risk detection plus a human-in-the-loop quality management system. Clinicians are in the monitoring and escalation path.
  • Be precise. Clinicians are not watching every conversation in real time. Talkspace says so explicitly.

08What it takes to do it right

One job. A human behind it.

Clinics using the AI chatbot+15%
Clinics without it+6%

Therapy sign-ups at NHS clinics, 20246

1st

That chatbot, Limbic, became the UK’s first approved mental-health AI device, 2023.

Give it one job · Keep a clinician close · Start in one clinic
  • Give it one job. Sign-ups, practice between sessions, or note-taking. Not acting as the therapist.
  • Keep a human close. If someone is at risk, a real clinician steps in right away, and the clinician makes the care decisions.
  • Start small, then check real results. One clinic first. Are people feeling better and getting care sooner?
  • After launch. Test on real patients across ages, languages and backgrounds. Keep monitoring and report problems. Protect the data and limit vendor use. Train staff on when not to trust it.
  • Include everyone. Other languages, and options for people without smartphones.
  • Seven areas experts say to check before deploying, privacy, safety and bias among them. Joint Commission and CHAI, 2025.

09Ethical and legal concerns

Four questions every tool has to answer

Do they know
it is a bot?

PrivacyHIPAA mostly does not cover appsCan your chats train the model?
HarmBias, and bots that always agreeFTC asked 7 firms about kids, 2025
BlameFDA only acts on treatment claimsCompany, therapist, or clinic?
APA · FTC · FDA · Joint Commission and CHAI
  • Disclosure. Always tell people they are talking to a bot. Get permission before recording a session for AI notes. State what it cannot do and where crisis help is.
  • Privacy. HIPAA, the main U.S. health privacy law, mostly does not cover apps. Since 2024 the FTC requires health apps to report data leaks. The open question is training and advertising use.
  • Harm. Models trained mostly on English, Western data misread other cultures. A bot that always agrees can reinforce a harmful thought. In 2025 the FTC asked seven AI companies, OpenAI and Meta included, how they protect children.
  • Accountability. The FDA only steps in when an app claims to treat an illness, so professional bodies like the APA are setting the standards instead.

16%Of that evidence base had follow-up. Anxiety benefit unproven
Promising, self-reported, and not approved as treatmentHRSA · NEJM AI · FDA
  • The upside. Roughly 1,600 depressed or anxious patients per U.S. provider. A bot is available at 2am, costs little, and has no waitlist.
  • Therabot RCT. n=210, large effects against a waitlist control, d≈0.9 depression and 0.8 anxiety, all self-reported.
  • Meta-analysis. 14 RCTs, n=6,314, moderate pooled effect on depression, g≈0.5, with wide error bars.
  • Engagement. People start sessions themselves, often at night, and rate the bond on par with human therapists.
  • Clinician support works too. AI note-taking cut burnout 21 points across 1,430 clinicians in general medicine. Administrative, not clinical.
  • The catch. Self-reported outcomes, waitlist controls, about 16% with follow-up, and zero FDA-authorized devices in the category.

13Discussion

Who decides
when you move up?

The user

The person in the worst shape is the least likely to escalate.

The risk model

The company selling the subscription sets the threshold.

The payer

Medicare has no AI policy, so the cheap layer stays out of pocket.

Tee has no waitlist and costs $19.99 a month
  • Frame it. If an AI guide is good enough for the first conversation, who decides when someone moves up to a licensed clinician?
  • The user decides. People already self-triage and a tool with no queue lowers the cost of asking early. But the person in the worst shape is the least likely to escalate, and the business earns more while they stay.
  • The risk model decides. It reads every conversation and the escalation path exists. But RAND found these models cannot grade mid-level risk, and the vendor sets the threshold.
  • The payer decides. Insurers can require a human after N sessions. But Medicare has no AI reimbursement policy, so incentives run to the cheapest option.
  • Our take. AI is good at conversation, paperwork and routing, and weak at judging risk. What works in 2026 picks one job and keeps a clinician attached to it.
